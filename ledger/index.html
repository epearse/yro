Year-Round Operations Ledger

A consolidated fiscal ledger, in actual dollars

An assessment of what "summer as a full semester" would cost based on publicly available data. The model is complex and highly sensitive to some parameters, and reasonable assumptions on the parameters (based on public data and previous experiments with summer operation in the CSU and elsewhere) could lead to extremely negative projections.

It is not the purpose of this document to convince anyone that these negative outcomes will occur. Rather, it is to determine the factors on which financial viability of YRO rests and understand the underlying assumptions.

This model and calculator were built by Claude and revised in response to hostile interrogation by ChatGPT for accuracy and fairness. It is based entirely on public data and the draft 14+1 calendar; sources are cited wherever they exist. An extensive discussion follows the calculator, providing methods, assumptions, sources, and various worked examples. All dollars are 2028-29 nominal unless otherwise noted. Compiled September 2026. It is a fiscal ledger rather than a full cost-benefit analysis: the educational and student benefits of the plan (added graduates, access to once-a-year courses, off-season internships) are reported where they arise but are not given a dollar value.

This model refers to five scenarios, which are used as presets in the calculator. The plan pays. This is not the administration’s actual plan (that plan has not been shared). This is one collection of settings that combines the values the administration has stated (45% of students on summer tracks, 31,800 distinct students a year with fall and spring at the ceiling, no recruitment premium) with various favorable settings for parameters it has not specified. Break even asks what it would take for the plan to pay without contradicting the evidence. Every setting with firm evidence behind it, or with little room to move, is left at the cautious scenario; the less-constrained settings are placed where they plausibly could sit, mostly to the plan’s advantage; and the number of students admitted on a full-cost (self-support) basis is then raised until the ten-year ledger clears zero, which takes about of them (Section 13). The central scenario is the one the faculty budget summary uses: the cautious scenario with about thirty settings moved in the plan’s favor, among them 500 full-cost admits, a 3% rather than 10% hiring premium, a $10M rather than $80M cooling retrofit, lower staffing, service and one-time costs, and no calendar, attrition or applicant-pool effects. All internal levers pulled comes from the cautious scenario by taking all eight settings the campus controls and pushing the value to the most favorable point in their documented ranges (Finding 3). I.e., everything set by Sacramento, the Trustees, the bargaining agreement or the construction market is left where the cautious scenario has it. The cautious scenario positions every slider at the best available estimate from public data, past history, and other reasons argued for in detail in Sections 5–13: sourced where a source exists, author-selected where none does, and labeled as such in each “Why?” popover.

Finding 1

Marginal summer student revenue: what the last student admitted at the design headcount contributes in a year, at the cautious scenario's track mix (revenue less what it costs to teach and serve them). The same student is worth under the assumptions under which the plan pays and under a stress case built from the CSU’s 2001 summer conversions. Under small perturbations, this figure may actually become negative!

The figure rests on:

  • distinct students a year: the 29,000 design applied to the programs on YRO, about two-thirds of enrollment
  • 40% of students on summer-inclusive tracks
  • 70% realization — off-cycle students who actually enroll in summer
  • 25,000 concurrent on-campus capacity per term
  • 15% of added students are non-resident
  • 35% of fee revenue not consumed by the service it funds
  • no new state growth appropriation for the added resident FTES
  • 15% of them funded through the CSU reallocation pool at $9,400 each
  • no students admitted on a full-cost basis
  • $3,000 of student services and overhead per added student
Finding 2
1/2

Fraction of students added to a summer session that bring new revenue when the campus is at capacity. Two summer seats are required for every new student YRO adds to a summer track. To make space for a new fall–spring student, one existing student must move to fall–summer and another must move to spring–summer. The summer term is therefore twice the size of the growth it makes possible, at any track share.

Moving a student from fall–spring to a summer track brings $0 in revenue but incurs a cost of – a year, depending on the other assumptions: under the assumptions under which the plan pays, at the cautious scenario, on a stress case built from the CSU’s 2001 summer conversions.

Most of the summer term is comprised of shifted students, not added students (86% at the cautious mix, 75–80% at SEM's projections) and each of those pays the same per-term tuition they would have paid in the term they gave up.

Section 3 · Course offerings

The increase in the number of course offerings, and associated costs

More offerings and smaller sections will increase costs and faculty workload.

The catalog states the terms in which each course is typically offered. In most departments, a substantial share of the upper-division curriculum runs in one regular term only: a specialized elective, a field course, the second half of a sequence. Today that is efficient, because every cohort is in phase and one full section a year serves everyone who needs it. Under YRO, a course offered once a year does not serve 1 of the 3 cohorts. This is not compliant with the vision that students have equitable access to courses regardless of cohort. This panel prices the increased cost to a department when sections multiply, enrollment per section falls, and the summer section has to be staffed by someone willing to teach it.

    5. What has actually been proposed

    In short The record of what has been announced, from the 2019 housing rationale to the current phased start: 23 programs across six colleges, the Orfalea College of Business in 2027-28 and the rest in 2028-29, and Strategic Enrollment Management’s two-of-three-semesters design.

    The plan has evolved considerably. It began in 2019 as a housing-relief measure, was announced in September 2023 as a 600-student pilot with "possibly 6,000" summer students at full build-out, slipped to summer 2025, then to 2027, and is now a phased start for 23 programs across six colleges after the campus completes its quarter-to-semester conversion in 2026-27: the Orfalea College of Business begins admitting summer-track cohorts in 2027-28, a year ahead of the published 2028 date, and the remaining programs follow in 2028-29.[4],[1] The current design, as described by Strategic Enrollment Management in October 2025, is that students enroll in two of three semesters: about 60% on a traditional fall–spring track, 20–25% on spring–summer, 20–25% on summer–fall (in practice fall–summer, since no entering student can start in summer), with no more than 85% of the student body on campus in any term and 40–50% of students in summer.[6] The 25,000, whether as Strategic Enrollment Management's 2030 target or as the Master Plan's ceiling that holds "until after 2035," counts students present on campus in a term. Under YRO the number of distinct students enrolled across a year would be larger than the number present in any one term, because each off-cycle student is absent for a term; that annual figure has not yet been published.

    6. How the money actually works

    In short Tuition and campus fees are charged per term of enrollment, and about 30% of tuition is set aside for aid. State marginal-cost funding is the swing variable and is not yet committed: Cal Poly already sits about 3.5% above its Chancellor’s Office allocation. Today’s summer, at the quarter pay rate, ran at roughly break-even on $12.9M.

    Why moving a student to summer brings no new revenue

    The intuition behind year-round operations is that buildings which sit unused for a third of the year should earn revenue against costs already being paid. The model does not find that result: a student's tuition, fees and state funding are the same whichever two terms they attend, so moving a term into summer earns nothing and costs the teaching, operations and transitions of a third term. The three-column table in Section 14 works this through one student at a time.

    Revenue per student does not depend on which terms the student attends

    CSU tuition is a flat per-term charge for anyone taking more than six units: $3,419 per semester in 2026-27, rising 6% a year through 2028-29 to about $3,841, or $7,683 for a two-term year.[13] Campus-based mandatory fees, the highest in the system at roughly $7,600 a year, are likewise charged per term of enrollment.[12] A student on a summer–fall track pays exactly what a fall–spring student pays. The summer term therefore generates revenue only to the extent it lets the university enroll students it otherwise could not fit. Roughly 30% of tuition is set aside for financial aid systemwide, and Cal Poly's own budget shows $52M in campus-based scholarships against $192M of tuition.[2] Half of the $8,804 Cal Poly Opportunity Fee paid by non-residents is redistributed as grants to Californians.

    State funding is the swing variable, and it is not yet committed

    The marginal-cost formula pays the state's share of about $11,296 per additional resident FTES in 2026-27, but only for growth the Chancellor's Office allocates and the Legislature funds.[13] Institutional Research's 2026-27 projection puts Cal Poly about 3.5% above its Chancellor's Office allocation of 19,161 resident FTES, one of ten campuses over target while thirteen are under.[2] The system has begun reallocating funds from under-enrolled to over-enrolled campuses, but at $32M for 3,397 FTES in 2025-26 (about $9,400 per FTES) and with the whole reallocation pool smaller than Cal Poly's proposed growth alone.[13] The 2025-26 budget deferred $144M of CSU's compact funding and the system faces a $2.3B structural shortfall; the marginal dollar for Cal Poly's growth is uncertain.[14] State money for added students comes through two doors that are not fiscally equivalent. New growth funded from the CSU's base increase pays the marginal-cost formula's state share ($11,296 per resident FTES for 2026-27, $10,983 in 2025-26, rising yearly; about $10,326 at Cal Poly after its own tuition is netted out) and is new money to the campus. Reallocation under the CSU's Enrollment Target and Budget Reallocation Plan moves existing target and funding from campuses below target to campuses above it, at a realised rate of about $8,200 in 2024-25 and $9,400 in 2025-26, from a pool capped by what the donor campuses lose: 3,891 FTES and $32M in 2024-25, 3,397 FTES and $32M in 2025-26, across the whole system.[43],[13] The calculator carries both as separate sliders. The cautious scenario assumes no new-growth appropriation and 15% of the added resident FTES funded by reallocation, about FTES, one more award the size of Cal Poly's largest to date; both are author-selected, and no growth funding for YRO has been announced. Scale is the reason for caution: the plan adds about resident FTES at one campus, against a Legislative Analyst's recommendation of 1% systemwide growth (about 3,500 FTES, $44M) for 2026-27, a Governor's expectation of 10,161 FTES to be met "from within the proposed base increases," and a reallocation mechanism that moved 3,397 FTES across the whole system in 2025-26. The Assembly's April 2026 budget agenda puts Cal Poly 6.2% over its 2024-25 target, which favors it for reallocation and also caps what reallocation can supply.[42] Four readings, with everything else at the cautious scenario:

    Financial need, the State University Grant, and what a needier intake changes

    About a third of each CSU tuition increase is set aside for the State University Grant, which covers tuition for students with financial need who are not covered by a Cal Grant: $49M of the $148M raised in 2024-25, $55M in 2025-26, $67M of $201M in 2026-27, for a pool of about $760M serving some 140,000 students.[44],[43],[13] The pool is systemwide and restricted, and the Chancellor's Office allocates it among campuses by their share of students with the lowest expected contributions, rebasing a small share each year with a floor on the prior year.[45] Cal Poly's allocation is $18.0M against $148M of resident tuition, about 12% where the system's is about 25%, because its Pell share, 23% in 2024, is the lowest in the CSU against a system share near 45%.[2],[46]

    Three consequences follow for the ledger. A larger Pell share does not raise revenue: a Pell student's tuition is paid by Pell, Cal Grant or the grant pool rather than by the family, and the campus books the same $7,683 either way; the grant is a restricted flow through the campus, not income to YRO. What a needier intake changes is cost, through above-average support and any supplemental aid the campus chooses to offer for fees and housing, and timing, because the grant allocation recognizes new need only through the annual rebasing. And the state's marginal-cost price already assumes a third of tuition goes to aid, so a needier funded student brings less family-paid tuition and, once the allocation has caught up, more General Fund; the two are not to be moved separately. The Financial need group in the calculator carries the Pell share of the added students, the support and aid increments beyond today's mix, and the share of the added grant need not yet recognized; at today's mix they add nothing, and the flag under the ledger reports the grant need. What a higher Pell share buys that the ledger cannot price is the case for state funding: growth at the campus with the system's lowest Pell share is the growth for which the case for state funding is weakest.

    The enrollment target is a commitment, and a shortfall against it carries a cost

    Since 2024-25 the CSU has run an Enrollment Target and Budget Reallocation Plan that moves enrollment target and the General Fund attached to it away from campuses below target and toward campuses at or above it. Five percent of a campus's FTES target and its associated marginal-cost funding is reallocated, and the threshold for being caught has tightened every year: more than 10% below target in 2024-25, more than 7% in 2025-26, more than 5% in 2026-27. By 2025-26, $64.2M and 7,288 FTES had moved through the mechanism, and about $26M more is planned for 2026-27.[41],[13],[14]

    Two features of that design bear directly on YRO. The first is that the reward and the penalty are not symmetric. A campus above target is paid out of a pool that is capped by what the campuses below target lose, shared among the ten campuses currently over, and priced at the reallocation rate rather than the marginal-cost formula: the plan funds reallocated and new growth at "between $7,900 and $8,900 General Fund per FTES" against a Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) state share of $10,983, and the realised rate was about $8,200 in 2024-25 and $9,400 in 2025-26. A campus below target loses 5% of its whole target, not 5% of the amount it missed by, and loses it from base budget rather than from growth it was hoping for. Upside is a share of a small pool; downside is a step.[41]

    The second is that growth is funded only if it is written into the target, and the target then becomes the number the campus is measured against. A campus that asks to be funded for YRO's enrollment is committing to deliver it. That is not a reason to avoid committing, but it does mean the downside case is not simply "YRO does not pay for itself." It is "YRO does not pay for itself and the campus is now below a target that YRO raised." The calculator carries both halves: how much of YRO's growth goes into the target, and what a shortfall against it costs. The penalty is dormant while the campus delivers its target, which is what the model does by default; it fires when the enrollment does not arrive, which is what the second reading of the realization slider in Section 9 describes. Cal Poly enters this with headroom, running about 3.5% above its current allocation, and the plan says explicitly that there is no penalty for exceeding target "as long as systemwide enrollment is below the system's funded target" — a condition that holds today, with thirteen campuses short by some 21,000 FTES, rather than a guarantee.[41],[2]

    Today's summer is roughly break-even at the quarter-system pay rate

    Cal Poly's FY2025-26 operating budget carries Summer 2025 as a separate column: 2,078 headcount, 1,099 FTES, $12.87M revenue, $12.91M expenditure.[2] That summer was taught by volunteers at the quarter rate of 1/45 of salary per WTU, in five- and ten-week sessions, with the administrative and student-service overhead of an already-open campus. It roughly broke even. Under semesters the volunteer rate becomes 1/30 (a 50% increase per unit), and the Registrar projects a 30% drop in summer headcount and units for 2026 from compressing to two sessions.[2],[10] At the semester pay rate, the starting point for an equal-stature summer is a term that runs at a loss before any of the YRO-specific costs are added.

    7. Who teaches summer

    In short Article 21 of the CFA–CSU agreement, the volunteer pool the evidence supports, and where the summer load actually lands: in service departments that are not on the YRO list and have no summer obligation under the contract.

    This is the load-bearing question, and the contract sets its terms. Article 21 of the CFA–CSU agreement governs summer term employment. Teaching in summer for extra pay "is voluntary," and the campus minimum share of summer work to be offered to probationary and tenured faculty (60% at San Luis Obispo, the highest in the system) is expressly "dependent upon a sufficient number of probationary and tenured faculty accepting the voluntary summer work assignments."[10] Academic-year appointments "explicitly exclude summer unless so specified in the appointment letter," and the only appointment-conversion language in the contract runs the other way, letting twelve-month counselors move to academic-year status.[10] Summer pay at a semester campus is 1/30 of annual salary per WTU plus $150 per unit of indirect instruction, reduced 5% for each student below twenty in a section. A full 15-WTU summer load for a $125,000 professor therefore costs about $65,000 plus a small benefits load, since retirement is not deducted from summer pay.[17]

    The administration's statements are consistent with the contract: faculty "will be able to choose which terms they will work," and summer teaching is "for additional compensation."[11],[5] Every YRO calendar since circulated makes summer a full fourteen-week term equal to fall and spring, so what a volunteer takes on is a full-term load paid at 1/30 of salary per WTU, not a compressed session. Nothing published indicates an agreement with CFA to make summer part of the base appointment, and the CBA's own year-round provisions (the "qualifying state-funded year-round operation" campuses, currently Humboldt and Sacramento) permit spreading workload across three terms only within the 180-workday academic year, which would thin fall and spring rather than add instruction.[10]

    How many existing faculty will volunteer? There is no Cal Poly survey. UC's 2000 feasibility study, the most careful institutional look at this question, found fewer than 20% of summer instructors were regular-rank faculty and those who taught averaged 1.1–1.4 courses.[16] Cal Poly's current summer, about 1,100 FTES, already uses about 61 FTEF-terms, roughly the 60 that a 15% participation rate at half load implies; the reconciliation beneath the volunteer slider shows the arithmetic. Every summer FTES beyond that has to be taught by lecturers on summer appointments or by newly hired faculty whose appointment letters specify summer. The calculator treats those as the three sources, in that order, and prices each.

    Two things about the staffing mix are worth having in front of the discussion. The first is that an equal-stature summer fixes it: fall and spring are about 37% lecturer-taught (465 of 1,265 FTEF), and a summer with a higher lecturer share would not be the equal of the regular terms, so the cautious scenario’s 40% is a parity assumption rather than a choice. The sweep below is a sensitivity; its rows at 60% and above are diagnostic, not compliant. The second is that at Cal Poly's salary levels the three sources of summer instruction rank in an order the contract does not anticipate: a tenure-line volunteer teaching for extra pay costs about $69,700 per FTEF-term, a summer lecturer about $48,000, and a new summer-obligated line about $134,500 per summer term net of the regular-term lecturer it displaces (about $115,600 with no premium). Under a parity constraint, volunteers are the cheapest compliant instruction and new lines the most expensive, so the volunteer rate is a first-order lever, though one that today's summer has already largely consumed.

    Where the summer load lands

    The hiring plan on the Academic Affairs page is organized by participating program. The summer load is not. For the first several years of phase-in the summer cohorts are first- and second-year students, and a first- or second-year in business, engineering, biology or kinesiology spends most of a term in courses taught by departments that are not on the YRO list: college algebra, business calculus, the calculus sequence, linear algebra and differential equations in Mathematics; the introductory sequences in Physics and Chemistry; statistics; composition and general education. At the design headcount of about summer students, with something like 60% of lower-division students in a mathematics course in any term, that is on the order of mathematics seats a summer, or about sections, in a department of about 40 tenure-line faculty whose volunteer pool at the rates the evidence supports covers a dozen. Physics, Chemistry and Statistics are in the same position in proportion.

    Three things follow. The volunteer capacity the campus can count on is smaller than a campus-wide average suggests, because a willing volunteer in Liberal Arts cannot teach business calculus. The lines needed first are in departments that are not participating in YRO, have no summer obligation under the contract, and are not the departments the hiring plan is written around. And the volunteer pool that covers the first year is spent by the second, when the surge arrives and the one-year hiring lead has not yet produced anyone. The calculator carries a slider for the share of summer instruction that is lower-division service, scopes the volunteer pool accordingly, and reports the implied service-department section count.

    What the new tenure lines encumber

    Because the CBA gives the administration no way to assign summer to existing faculty, the plan's instruction has to be hired, and the Academic Affairs page says searches will begin "approximately one year in advance of needed instruction."[1] In the calculator's cautious scenario that is about new tenure-line positions at roughly each in salary, premium and benefits: a year of base budget, committed before the students it serves have enrolled, on a campus that drew $34.6M from reserves to close FY2025-26.[2] Recruiting those lines without a premium is a permissible favorable sensitivity, not the planning case, until the campus has discipline-level search and acceptance evidence. A tenure line is a thirty-year obligation; the present value of of them at 5% is roughly , plus startup packages in engineering and science that run into six figures each. The decision-useful figure is the a year.

    Encumbered funds are the opposite of flexibility. If summer enrollment tracks the CSU's own history (an 88% jump in year one, a 37% decline by year four), the tenure lines remain. They cannot be shed except through Article 38 layoff, which the CSU has essentially never used for tenured faculty, so the shortfall comes out of the parts of the budget that can move: lecturer sections, operating budgets, deferred maintenance, and the discretionary programs (Teacher-Scholar, tenure density in existing departments, graduate support) that the revenue was supposed to fund. The same lines also have an opportunity cost, and it is worth stating: if the campus is going to commit this money, here is what else it could buy. Fall and spring instruction today is delivered by roughly 800 tenure-line FTEF (full-time-equivalent faculty: total teaching workload divided by a full 15-WTU load, so two half-time lecturers count as one) and 465 lecturer FTEF per term, a tenure density near 63%. The same hires, directed at ordinary fall–spring appointments, would replace about of the lecturer FTEF with tenure lines and lift tenure density to roughly , a goal both the Senate and CFA have named as a priority, for the same (less, with no summer-recruitment premium), without creating a single summer obligation and without depending on summer enrollment ever materializing. YRO and tenure density compete for the same dollars and the same hiring capacity; choosing one constrains the other. And the hires themselves are a bet on the labor market: the plan asks candidates in computer science, engineering and business, who have industry alternatives and offers from campuses that leave summer free, to accept an appointment letter that specifies summer teaching. Either the campus pays a premium, or it recruits from a thinner pool, or the summer clause goes unenforced in practice, at which point the instruction problem returns.

    The premium has a second cost that arrives later. A new summer-obligated assistant professor at $115,000 plus a 10% premium starts at about $126,500; the IPEDS averages for Cal Poly are $106,000 for assistants and $120,000 for associates, so the new hire would out-earn the typical existing assistant in the same department by about $20,000 and the typical tenured associate by about $6,500.[17] That is salary inversion, and it concentrates in the departments that receive the hires. It is corrected the way inversions usually are, a few years on, through equity adjustments, retention offers or salary grievances, and because the correction goes into base pay it compounds with every general salary increase afterward; the premium also travels with the new hire through promotion, so the gap does not close on its own. The ledger carries this as a count of existing faculty brought up by the premium. At 250, about half the campus's assistant and associate professors, it adds roughly $4.7M a year, arriving from the third year in the ten-year view. The alternative to paying the premium is the thinner candidate pool described above; the inversion cost is the price of the premium working.

    8. Facilities, labs, and the summer construction window

    Cal Poly Facilities Management states that "some buildings have only heating service and are not equipped with mechanical ventilation and cooling. Many of our buildings have only ventilation."[18] Executive Order 987 bars using utility funds to cool spaces below 78°F even where cooling exists. San Luis Obispo's coastal averages (a 78°F mean high in August) understate the inland-valley campus and the heat-event tail; students already list air conditioning among their objections.[7] No building-by-building inventory is public. The calculator carries a cooling-retrofit capital line, annualized over thirty years, that can be set to zero if summer instruction can be confined to the buildings that already have cooling.

    The deeper facilities issue is the calendar itself. Every summer, Cal Poly schedules its major construction and maintenance "for the convenience of a lower campus population"; the 2025 summer list ran from the library to the water reclamation facility to the housing projects that growth depends on.[19] The president noted the conflict in 2023.[4] A campus occupied at 40–50% in summer does not lose the ability to do that work; it pays a premium for it (phasing, nights and weekends, longer schedules), and it does so against a CSU capital-renewal backlog of $8.6 billion.[13] Lab-based and studio-based programs (all eleven engineering majors, architecture, construction management, animal science, biology, kinesiology: most of the 23 programs on the planning list) have no between-term window for restocking, equipment turnover, and technician leave if summer is a full term. And the space a summer term makes available is not the space that is short: the LAO's 2017 utilization study found CSU classrooms at 89% of the state guideline but teaching labs at 105%, already over.[15] Summer adds lecture seats to a polytechnic whose binding constraint is bench space.

    Summer housing: the beds that fill

    The strongest financial argument for a full summer term is not in the instructional ledger at all. University Housing is the one part of the campus that reliably makes money: $146.7M of revenue against $96.8M of expenditure in 2024-25, a $49.9M surplus before debt service, the largest in the CSU, on 8,517 occupied spaces at 96.8% occupancy, above design capacity.[50] Those beds are full in fall and spring whatever happens, because demand exceeds supply, and they are largely empty in summer apart from conferences and camps. A summer term puts students in them. The campus says as much in its bond disclosures: the $280.8M of new housing principal issued since mid-2025 is to be serviced by keeping roughly 8,500 beds full and fed, including, from 2027, in the summer (see the companion debt report).

    The ledger therefore credits each added summer resident with a term's net margin. Two sliders set it. The first is the share of added summer students who take a campus bed (slider ): all first- and second-years must live on campus,[51] so in the phase-in years nearly every summer student is a resident, and at steady state, when the summer cohort spans all four years, the share settles between the campus-wide 36% and about half; the cautious value is 50%. The second is the net margin per resident-term (slider ): a semester of housing bills about $6,600, the marginal cost of a summer term in a building whose debt, staff and renewal are already paid by fall and spring is on the order of $2,000, and dining and parking add a few hundred dollars; the cautious value is $5,000. At the cautious scenario that is added summer residents and a year, against the $2M of forgone conference income charged beside it, and it replaces the flat $1M placeholder the ledger carried before. Under the assumptions under which the plan pays, with about 12,900 summer students, the credit is a year.

    Two cautions. The credit depends on the bed stock being idle in summer, which it is today; if the campus fills summer beds with conference business at a comparable margin, the slider above and the forgone-income slider cancel. And it depends on the housing requirement following the student into summer, which means per-term rather than academic-year licences; the calendar slider below then becomes a question of whether the per-term price is held at today's per-semester level or pro-rated to fourteen weeks.

    The shorter calendar and the auxiliaries

    The 14+1 calendar that would make three terms fit in a year takes a week from each regular term, so a student on a fall–spring track is on campus two fewer weeks a year. That is the debit side of a two-sided housing ledger, and it is easy to misread on its own. Per bed, the calendar costs two weeks of occupancy a year; a summer occupant adds fourteen. The campus-wide total therefore rises whenever summer residents exceed one-seventh of fall residents. At the cautious scenario the 8,500 fall and spring residents give up about bed-weeks and the summer residents add , a net gain of bed-weeks ( of today's occupancy); under the plan-pays assumptions the gain is , and under a stress case built from the CSU’s 2001 summer conversions, with few summer residents, the stock loses . In dollars the two sides net to a year at the cautious scenario: the summer credit above less the calendar cost below.

    The calendar side is priced by slider . Housing, dining and parking are self-support auxiliaries whose revenue follows occupancy: University Housing budgeted $108.7M of revenue on about 8,400 residents in 2024-25, Cal Poly Partners $62M (chiefly dining) and parking $6.6M in permit fees.[2] If every one of those dollars scaled with weeks and none of the cost did, the fall and spring loss would be about $10M a year; that is the ceiling, not the estimate. What the slider really measures is pass-through: under YRO housing licences will be sold by the term, since summer-track students are on campus in different terms, and a per-term price equal to today's per-semester price loses nothing (students pay about 7% more per week), while a per-week price loses the full amount; dining plans will be re-priced for fewer weeks, but about half of dining revenue is food and labor cost that falls with them; parking permits are priced per term. The ledger carries the fall/spring side as a standing cost with a cautious value of $3M a year and a plausible range of $1.5M to $5M, charged in full from the first YRO year because the calendar changes for every student at once. It is a cost of the calendar rather than of the summer term, and it should be set to zero if the campus keeps 73-day terms.

    9. Will students come?

    In short The randomized evidence on summer enrollment, UC’s finding that 70% of undergraduates work in summer, the CSU’s own 2001 conversions, Dartmouth and Waterloo, and the UNSW reversal. Also the Fall–Summer first year: arrive in August, leave in December, return in May.

    The design assumes that 40–50% of students will enroll in summer because they agreed to a track at admission. The evidence on voluntary summer enrollment points to low uptake, and the evidence on assigned tracks is thin. In the only randomized study, a free summer course raised community-college summer enrollment from 33% to 53%; students who declined cited work and time, not cost.[21] UC found that 70% of its undergraduates work in the summer and estimated that even a fully funded summer would reach "at best" 40% of fall.[16] Nationally, 47% of students ever take a summer class over six years, earning about 9 credits in total, roughly 7% of a degree.[22] When California paid to convert four CSU campuses to state-funded summer in 2001, summer enrollment rose 88% in the first year, then fell 37% at those campuses by 2004, and by 2015 CSU summer enrollment systemwide was 2.1% of fall against a statutory target of 40%.[20],[15] Cal Poly itself was a year-round campus from the late 1960s; in 1999 its summer ran at 15% of fall, the lowest of the four CSU year-round campuses.[23]

    The institutions that do fill summer do it with a required term or a co-op structure. Dartmouth requires sophomore summer of a fixed 4,500-student cohort and pairs it with mandatory off-terms; Waterloo assigns engineering students to streams and relies on employers to absorb half the cohort in any given term.[24] Cal Poly engineering students asked: "where are they going to get 2,000 co-ops?"[7] The 1966 trimester wave (Pittsburgh, SUNY Binghamton, the Florida system) ran into the same difficulties and reverted within a few years.[27]

    YRO and the calendar alignment semesters were meant to provide

    One of the reasons given for converting to semesters, at a cost of $20M, was calendar alignment. The Semester Conversion FAQ says the new calendar "will make it easier to pursue summer jobs, internships, or research opportunities, as employers often align their start dates with the end of a spring semester," and lists "summer internship start and end dates" among the student-success gains the Chancellor cited.[29] That benefit is real, and under YRO it survives only for the 50–60% of students on the traditional track. A summer–fall student is in class from June through December; a spring–summer student from January through August. Neither is free in the May-to-August window that employers, and the semester calendar, are built around. Their free term is fall or spring, when firms hire few interns, campus career fairs are aimed at the following summer, and the recruiting cycle (applications in fall for summer positions) runs against them.

    So for students on off-cycle tracks, the alignment benefit is lost rather than preserved. The traditional track keeps the semester benefit unchanged; the off-cycle tracks end up worse off than they were under quarters, when at least a June-to-September window existed. Co-op universities such as Waterloo, Northeastern and Drexel solve this with employer-relations operations that place students in off-cycle work terms by the thousands, built over decades; Cal Poly's interim engineering dean has described co-ops, study abroad and "study abroad at the hangar" as possibilities that are still being worked out.[7] Until that infrastructure exists, the alignment that semester conversion was meant to provide would not reach the 40–50% of students on off-cycle tracks.

    Transfer students

    The semester conversion was presented in part as a transfer measure: an aligned calendar with the community colleges and the other 22 CSU campuses, better articulation, and a more "transfer-friendly" institution. Under YRO, transfer students are the group the design fits least well.

    • They cannot start in summer. Under the 2027-28 calendar the summer term begins in early May, before any California community college has finished its spring semester or posted grades. A transfer admitted to a summer track has no transcript to admit on; the Instruction Committee's finding was that transfers "would not be able to enroll in a summer start but would have to wait until fall." The summer/fall track is closed to them at entry, and a spring/summer start has the same problem in reverse.
    • They have the fewest terms left, in the part of the curriculum YRO fragments. A transfer arrives with about 60 units and four to five terms to go, all of it upper-division: the courses offered once a year, the once-a-year electives, the two-term capstone. An off-cycle transfer meets the wait-a-year problem immediately and has no slack to absorb it. Cal Poly's transfer two-year graduation rate is already 37% against a Graduation Initiative target of 45%; every term lost to a course that is not offered in their term comes straight off that number.
    • The alignment they were offered runs the other way. Community colleges, the CSU and UC run fall and spring. A transfer on a summer track leaves a semester calendar for one that no other public institution in the state uses, cannot take a needed course at a home community college or another CSU in an off term because the terms do not line up, and holds a 12-month lease for eight months of residence.
    • The natural fix is to keep transfers on the traditional track, which is what programs will likely do. But the traditional track is the one being reduced to make room for the summer cohorts, so transfer admission and first-year summer-track admission compete for the same fall/spring seats, and transfers, who bring the least tuition per remaining term and whom the CSU is asking campuses to admit more of, are the ones under pressure.

    The Fall–Summer first year

    Summer classes begin in early May under every YRO calendar circulated, before any high school has graduated its seniors, so no entering student can start in summer. The two off-cycle tracks therefore take a particular shape for freshmen. The summer–fall track becomes Fall–Summer: a new student arrives in August, leaves in December, is away from January to May, and returns as the summer term opens and the students they met in the fall depart. The spring–summer track begins with a gap semester after high school and a January start. Every entering student begins in fall or spring, and the summer term is populated entirely by continuing students; the first summer cohort in 2028 would be the fall-2027 entrants returning after four months away.

    The retention literature is consistent that first-year persistence is built in the first semester, through friendships, housing, clubs and a first advisor, and that a break after one semester behaves like a stop-out: students who leave campus after a single term re-enroll at lower rates than students who stay continuously, even when the leave was planned. Dartmouth, the one American example of a required summer term, keeps first-year students in residence for fall, winter and spring and places the required summer in the sophomore year; the calendar was designed around exactly this risk.[24] A Fall–Summer freshman has no campus anchor from January to May, and the rational thing to do in that gap is to enroll at a community college at home, which keeps the student busy, costs little, and moves units that would have been taken at Cal Poly off its FTES count.

    The pattern also matters for yield. As an admissions offer, arrive in August, leave in December, return in May is the least attractive of the three tracks, so it will fill furthest down each program's ranked list, and it is the track on which a full-cost seat (Section 13) is most likely to be offered. For housing, a Fall–Summer student occupies a room for one term, vacates it in December, and needs one again in May; the room is turned over twice a year instead of once, with the cleaning, inspection, re-keying and move-in staffing that implies, and financial aid, which is disbursed by term and depends on enrollment status, has to be handled across a planned semester away. Housing pays for its own turnover through rates, which is to say the residents pay for it; the ledger does not count it. What the ledger does count is a first-year attrition rate specific to this track, separate from the continuing off-cycle rate, and a per-term transition cost for the enrollment, orientation and aid work that three entry points a year create.

    What happened at UNSW

    The most recent and best-documented reversal is the University of New South Wales in Sydney, a research university of about 60,000 students. In 2019 UNSW replaced its two semesters with a "3+" calendar of three ten-week terms plus a shorter summer session, for the reasons Cal Poly now gives: the campus "lay largely vacant for around five months a year," and a third term would let students accelerate or work while keeping progress toward a degree. In April 2025, after consulting about 5,000 students and 1,000 staff, the university announced it would abandon trimesters, transitioning by 2028 to a "flex-semester" calendar of two twelve-week semesters with optional six-week summer and winter terms, explicitly aligned "with school holidays and job recruitment patterns."[25] That replacement is, in substance, the calendar Cal Poly will have in 2026-27 before YRO.

    The complaints that ended the experiment correspond closely to the concerns raised about Cal Poly's plan. Students reported reduced access to summer employment, insufficient time to engage with course material in a compressed term, fewer and shorter breaks (student activists called them "crymesters" and marched under "more university, less holidays"), and misalignment with northern-hemisphere calendars that complicated exchange and international recruitment. Staff reported increased workloads, reduced sabbatical opportunities, and research time crowded out by a third teaching term. The vice-president who announced the change, Verity Firth, said the trimester model "was very much of its time" and that students wanted to "engage more deeply with course material." Six years and two curriculum redesigns, one to adopt the calendar and one to undo it: that is what the experiment cost.

    The calculator's "realization" slider is the share of off-cycle-track students who actually enroll in summer as planned. What happens to the rest is a modelling choice with real consequences, and the calculator carries both readings. If the track is a preference, the student enrols anyway and attends fall and spring, which is what the record shows where summer has been voluntary, and which is what pushes concurrent enrollment back toward the cap. If the track is a condition of admission, which is how SEM describes assigning tracks at admission, an applicant who will not spend a summer on campus does not enrol at all: the seat goes unfilled and the campus falls short of its target headcount by the unrealized share. The second reading is the more consequential one, because a student who drifts into fall and spring still pays tuition and fees while a student who goes elsewhere pays nothing, and the summer term is built either way. There is a third possibility the model does not carry, which the campus would likely reach for first: backfilling the declined seat from the waiting list with an applicant who will take summer. That is closer to raising realization than to either setting, and its cost is paid in admissions selectivity rather than in dollars.

    The applicant pool after 2025

    California's public high-school graduates peaked in 2025. The Department of Finance projects about 424,800 in 2026-27, a plateau through 2029-30, and about 381,300 by 2035-36, 16% below 2024-25; WICHE's longer projection is 29% below 2023 by 2041, with Hispanic graduates falling least and white graduates most.[47],[48] YRO launches on the plateau and matures in the decline, after the tenure lines and the cooling retrofit are in place. The system is already responding: new resident freshmen fell 3% in fall 2025, fourteen campuses admitted at least 90% of resident freshman applicants against three in 2019, thirteen missed their 2024-25 targets, and the answer has been mostly to move funded enrollment to the campuses that can fill it rather than to appropriate growth.[13]

    Cal Poly is impacted in every program, with 71,171 applicants, 20,513 selected and a target class of 5,500 in 2025, so a smaller pool does not empty it.[49] It reaches the plan through the margins. A class about a thousand larger at today's yield takes about 24,000 offers, 38–43% of a pool 10–20% smaller; at 18% yield it takes 36,000, 57–64% of it. The campus keeps its numbers and gives up selectivity, control of the major mix and bargaining power over price and calendar, and the Fall–Summer offer, which fills furthest down each program's list, is where the thinning arrives first. Today a declined summer-track offer is refilled from the waitlist, which is why the realization slider's default reading has decliners attending fall and spring; as the qualified pool thins in the smaller programs the seat stays empty, and the slider comes to mean what its enroll-elsewhere reading says. The Dynamics group carries the pool's annual change and two sensitivities, zero by default because no estimate exists: how fast realization falls with the pool, and how many declined offers go unfilled. The scenario matrix after the calculator runs both.

    10. How many more students, really

    In short Graduates per four years under five scenarios. YRO earns its throughput by serving 29,000–33,000 distinct students a year on a campus with 25,000 present at a time — a sixth to a third more students than any published figure, and every cost scales with the larger number.

    The question that matters is not how fast any one student finishes but how many the university graduates every four years. That number is set by how many students are enrolled in a year: Cal Poly awarded 6,705 degrees to 6,312 graduates in 2024 on about 23,000 students, roughly one graduate per 3.7 enrolled students, so four-year output is about 4 × annual headcount ÷ 3.7.[28] Today the annual count and the fall count are the same number. YRO changes the output only by making the annual count larger than the number present in any one term, which is the whole of its capacity argument. Everything therefore turns on what happens to concurrent enrollment.

    Two things follow
    • If the annual count stayed at 25,000, the summer term would add no graduates at all. The same 25,000 students graduate at the same rate whether they attend fall–spring or summer–fall, and 25,000 fits under the per-term ceiling without a summer term, which is how the campus has been growing (the Senate Executive Committee was told a 0.1-unit rise in average load alone yields 145 FTES). That is why the design grows the annual count.[8]
    • YRO earns its graduates by serving 29,000–33,000 distinct students a year on a campus with 25,000 present at a time. That honors the per-term ceiling the president gave the community, and it is the design as described; but it is a sixth to a third more students served in a year than any published figure, and the summer term, the tenure lines, the housing turnover and the city's year-round occupancy all scale with the larger number, in a city where the housing plan is already the largest capital project in campus history.[4]

    Whether the campus gets emptier depends on which number holds

    SEM's expectation that concurrent enrollment falls holds only if the annual count is held at 25,000: then fall and spring each hold 75–80% of it, 19,400–20,000 students, three to four thousand fewer than today, and the faculty, technicians and advisors who serve 23,400 in fall and spring would serve fewer while a summer term with none of them contracted served 10,000. The design avoids that by growing the annual count until fall and spring are back at the ceiling. But that relocates the staffing question rather than resolving it. Fall and spring then hold exactly the 25,000 the campus is already planning for, taught by the faculty it already has, and the summer term serves 8,000 students (14,500 at SEM's full design) with no one contracted to teach them. The plan does not add capacity to the terms the campus already runs; it adds a third term's worth of new capacity, in the one term for which no faculty are currently appointed, and fills it with students who would otherwise have needed seats in the first two. The balance is fragile in both directions: if realization falls short, students drift back and fall and spring overflow the ceiling; if the campus over-admits to the summer tracks, the regular terms thin out and lecturer appointments fall, and the calculator reports how many when that happens.

    For impacted courses that means sections move, and fragment.

    Example scenarioTake a required course that enrolls 1,200 students a year in twenty sections of thirty each fall and spring. Under the cautious scenario, fall and spring demand rises about 7% (to twenty-one sections each, since the regular terms are back at the ceiling) and summer needs seven sections, in a term the same length as fall and spring, of a course that may have a lab. Under SEM's design, fall and spring hold at twenty-one and summer needs twelve. The total number of sections rises 20–35% for a 24–38% rise in annual headcount, roughly proportional; but every added section is in summer, and the faculty who could teach it are contracted for fall and spring. The department must either persuade its own tenure-line faculty to add summer sections on top of full fall and spring loads, which the contract makes voluntary and which the available evidence suggests few will do, or hire new faculty for summer. The answer to "would we need to reduce fall and spring sections of impacted courses to offer them in summer" is therefore no at the design headcount, and yes by roughly the share of students away each term if the annual count is held at 25,000; either way the summer sections are new, and no one is yet contracted to teach them.

    The fragmentation is worse for anything not offered every term. A junior-level lab or a specialized elective offered once a year today serves a single in-phase cohort. With three cohorts out of phase, it must be offered in a second or a third term, and the added summer section draws only the off-cycle students (28% of the course's annual demand in the cautious scenario, half that if the course runs every term), which raises cost per student and triggers the CBA's 5%-per-student pay reduction below twenty in summer; or it stays annual, and the cohort whose two terms never include that one cannot take it at all without a third term, a substitution or a change of track, which lengthens time to degree and cuts against the throughput claim.     Finally, the students in the 40–50% off-cycle share have to be retained. Off-cycle engineering students lose the internship summer that their employers hire for; more than 80% of those surveyed say YRO will harm Cal Poly and some say they would leave.[7] The calculator prices that with two sliders, one for the Fall–Summer first year and one for continuing off-cycle students. Seats can be refilled, so revenue holds, but every student who leaves rather than take a summer track is a graduate the four-year count does not get.

    There is a quieter version of the same problem in the departments that are not on YRO at all. Physics, mathematics, statistics and most of the liberal arts will teach the service sequences that participating programs' summer cohorts need, without themselves being on year-round operations and with no obligation under the contract to do so. Their upper-division curricula, which is where minors, second majors and changes of major live, will keep running once a year on the fall/spring cycle. A summer-track engineering student who wants a mathematics minor, or who is struggling and wants to change into physics, arrives on a calendar the receiving department does not run. Change of major is one of the main ways students who falter in engineering stay at Cal Poly instead of leaving; narrowing it for a third of each participating cohort has a cost in graduates that the calculator carries as a share of off-cycle students blocked each year, of whom most wait a term and some leave.

    What the versions of the calendar do

    The YRO calendars compress in different places, and each costs something. The 2024 draft, and the 14+1 draft that repeats it, shortened the terms: 12–14 fewer instructional days a year, more than 50 over a degree, "the equivalent of one 10-week quarter of instruction," with contact hours in a three-unit lecture falling from 45 to 42 and, because lab sections cannot be run in partial weeks, the loss of two lab periods a semester in courses that were already merged for the semester curriculum on the assumption of fifteen sessions.[34] The 2027-28 proposal keeps the days and removes the breaks instead: two days between spring and summer, six between summer and fall, seventeen weekdays off between the start of fall and commencement against twenty-nine today, all-hands workdays on weekends, and no window in which equipment-based labs can be repaired, classrooms refreshed, or a course prepared for a new cohort.[37] Either version puts the same pressure on the same place: the lower-division sequences in physics, chemistry, mathematics and engineering that were designed for fifteen weeks, that feed every program on the YRO list, and whose accreditors count contact hours. ABET does not prescribe a term length, but it audits whether a program's curriculum delivers its stated outcomes, and a department that has just documented a fifteen-week design to WSCUC and ABET will have to defend delivering it in fourteen. The School of Education's problem is arithmetic rather than judgment: 630 planned clinical hours become 563 on a 13–14 week term, against a state requirement of 600.[34]

    The concern faculty raised in 2024 is consistent with the literature. The ASIC report said that reducing instruction while holding outcomes fixed means "students will need to learn more material on their own, increasing their stress and likely resulting in higher DFW rates," and that if faculty instead reduce outcomes, "the value of a Cal Poly degree will necessarily be reduced."[34] The best causal evidence on calendar change is Bostwick, Fischer and Lang's study of 132 colleges that switched calendars, co-authored by a Cal Poly economist: the switch lowered four-year graduation rates by 2.9 percentage points nationally and 4.4 in Ohio transcript data, with nearly half of the effect coming from a 2.6-point rise in first-year dropout, driven by lower first-year grades and lighter course loads, and the effects did not fade after the transition.[38] Cal Poly is about to switch calendars once, in 2026, and YRO proposes switching again in 2028. On compressed terms specifically, the short-term evidence is mixed: intensive courses earn equal or better ratings and midterm scores, but Gustafson and Lim found traditional-length students did better on finals requiring comprehension and analysis, and nobody has studied retention of cumulative STEM content across a compressed sequence.

    What a D/F costs the system

    A D or F in a sequence course is not a private event. The student retakes it, which consumes a seat in a course that is already impacted, delays every course downstream, and adds a term to the degree; a student who fails twice, or who fails physics in the first year, tends to leave. Each of those has a price the campus pays: the seat has to be taught, the added term is a term in which the student occupies a place that a new admit would otherwise fill (so graduates per year fall even though headcount does not), and the departure is a graduate the four-year count never gets. The calculator carries these as a labeled downside layer: off in the steady state by default, because no estimate specific to the implemented calendar exists, and applied to the first three cohort-years in the ten-year view, when the first cohorts will meet courses designed for fifteen weeks delivered in fourteen. The Calendar & instructional quality group in Section 1 has sliders for the instructional days in a regular term under the YRO calendar, the rise in D/F/W rates in affected courses, the share of enrollments in those courses, the extra first-year attrition the change causes, and the one-time cost of redesigning the curriculum a third time. With the layer on at the cautious defaults (73 days, a 3-point rise in D/F/W across 40% of enrollments, half of it absorbed by grading, one point of extra first-year attrition) the calendar adds about 1,700 retake seats a year, $1.7M of instruction, and a measurable loss of graduates; with nothing absorbed, twice that. Set the days to 67 and the D/F/W rise to 5 to see the 2024 draft.

    The redesign is priced because it will not happen for free: faculty spent two years rebuilding every course for semesters, the campus paid stipends for that work, and a third rebuild would reasonably be compensated the same way.

    13. A related proposal: full-cost admission

    In short Admitting students at full cost, through self-support tracks or non-resident rates for Californians. It is the only single lever on this page that closes the YRO gap without state action, which is why the two proposals are worth testing together. Five unanswered questions, and the UC precedent.

    Read the full section

    There is a second way to make the summer term pay that does not depend on Sacramento: admit some of the added students on a full-cost basis. It has two forms. One is the proposal, reported to faculty but not published, that California applicants who fall below a major's Multi-Criteria Admission (MCA) cutoff be admitted anyway if they pay non-resident tuition and fees.[30] The other, which departmental planning indicates is how the first business cohorts will be enrolled, is a self-support track: students admitted to the same major on the same calendar but through Extended Education at full cost, outside the state-supported enrollment count. The arithmetic of the two is the same; the law and the questions they raise differ. Nothing describing either has been published on the admissions, enrollment or Academic Affairs pages; what follows tests them as described and should be revised when a written description is available.

    The question worth testing is whether full-cost admission is functioning as part of YRO's financing. The arithmetic suggests the two are linked, for four reasons.

    • It is the only revenue line that arrives at the right size. A regular resident student nets the campus roughly $5,400 in tuition after aid plus a few thousand in fees, and state marginal-cost funding only if the Chancellor's Office allocates it. A full-cost student, whether a Californian paying non-resident rates or a self-support admit at roughly $500–700 a unit, nets about $26,000 before fees, with no dependence on Sacramento. In the calculator, at the cautious settings, each seat filled by a full-cost admit rather than a state-supported resident improves the annual result by about $16,400: the full-cost charge less the resident tuition and state funding the seat would otherwise have brought. The cost of teaching and serving the student is the same either way.
    • It adds revenue, not capacity. The programs on the YRO list were reportedly chosen because they turn away large numbers of MCA-qualified applicants. If so, the summer-track seats can be filled with qualified Californians at resident rates and there is no capacity reason to admit anyone at full cost; the only thing a full-cost admit adds is the difference between resident and full-cost charges. The break-even count is therefore a dollars calculation: at the cautious scenario the ledger is about a year short, closing it takes roughly full-cost admits (about a year), and each one takes a seat that would otherwise go to a resident at state-supported rates.
    • Yield is where the pressure appears. In the fill-to-cap pattern, a third of each participating program's admitted class must accept an offer that includes summer. Every qualified applicant who declines pushes the program one place further down its ranked list. A program with a deep qualified pool absorbs that; a program with a thin one reaches the cutoff, and admitting the next applicant at full cost fills the seat at a premium. That is the mechanism by which a pricing arrangement could become an admissions practice, whether or not anyone intends it.
    • The two depend on each other. With fall and spring already at the Master Plan's 25,000-per-term ceiling, thousands of additional students can only be present in summer, or in seats that have to be built. Without a summer term there is nowhere to put them; without their tuition the summer term does not pay. Move the slider at the bottom of the Revenue group and watch the critical flag under the ledger report how many such admits the current settings require.

    The non-resident-rate variant: the legal question

    Education Code section 68050 requires "a student classified as a nonresident" to pay non-resident tuition; it does not authorize charging non-resident tuition to a resident, and the Trustees set non-resident tuition under a statutory scheme built entirely on residency classification.[31] A California resident who agrees to pay the non-resident rate would be paying a charge the code does not appear to contemplate for residents, and the Cal Poly Opportunity Fee was justified to the Trustees and the community explicitly as a fee on non-residents, with half of its proceeds regranted to Californians. The established route to charging Californians full cost is self-support, which is the second variant.

    The self-support variant: what it raises

    A self-support track admits students to a state-supported major through Extended Education at a per-unit fee that recovers full cost, the way summer session and a number of graduate programs are already priced. It is lawful on its face, and it is the form the first business cohorts appear to take. It raises five questions that have not yet been answered. Supplanting: CSU policy bars self-support programs from replacing or displacing state-supported instruction, and a self-support cohort in an impacted major, taking the same courses in the same sections as state-supported students, would need to be reconciled with that rule. Revenue: self-support revenue is retained by the offering college, with the departments that teach the service courses, which is most of the instruction for a first- or second-year, reimbursed on a cost-recovery basis; the mathematics sections that a business cohort generates are taught by Mathematics and paid for by Business. Instructor pay: self-support instruction is paid under Extended Education's terms rather than Article 21's state-support rate, and for tenure-line faculty it is additional employment under the 125% rule; whether the rate is the same, and who sets it, has not been stated. Counting: self-support students are not state-supported FTES, earn no marginal-cost funding and do not count toward the enrollment target the campus is already over, so a summer term filled with them does nothing for the state's capacity goals. Price: two students in the same major, admitted in the same year, sitting in the same section, would pay about $7,000 and about $22,000 for the same seat, and the one paying more is the one admitted to the track with the thinner elective menu and no internship summer. The Legislative Analyst has raised concerns before about self-support undergraduate offerings at the CSU as tuition increases by another name; the same concern is likely here, which is why the fee, the revenue split, the instructor pay terms and the supplanting analysis belong in the data request before the first cohort enrolls.

    The precedent

    The University of California's experience with a neighboring version is instructive. In 2016 the State Auditor found that UC, to raise revenue, had relaxed its standard for non-residents from "the upper half of residents" to "compare favorably," admitted nearly 16,000 non-residents with scores below the median of admitted residents at the same campus over three years, and denied nearly 4,300 residents whose scores met or exceeded every median for non-residents, while non-resident supplemental tuition grew from $325M to $728M a year.[32] The Legislature responded by capping non-resident enrollment. That case concerned non-residents admitted for revenue. The non-resident-rate variant would admit Californians below a faculty-set standard on the basis of ability to pay, in majors where the same standard is applied to other Californians; the self-support variant would admit them at the standard but at about three times the price. Either turns an academic threshold into a price, at a campus that already has the widest gap between resident and non-resident charges in the system and has drawn scrutiny over the Opportunity Fee.

    What the numbers say

    The calculator cannot speak to intent; it can say what the arrangement does. Under the plan-pays preset (state funds 30% of the growth as new money, 90% realization, no premium for summer hires), YRO shows a surplus without full-cost admits, and neither variant would be necessary. Under the cautious assumptions, YRO does not pay, and full-cost admission is the one single lever on the page that closes the gap without state action (the eight internal levers of Finding 3, pulled together, reach ). The test is therefore simple: run the YRO financial model with the full-cost revenue line removed. If the case survives, the two proposals are independent. If it does not, the calendar and the pricing arrangement are a single decision and should be discussed as one.

A reader who believes every revenue assumption here is far too pessimistic is still left with $3,647 per summer-track student per year, because the correction they would make applies equally to the alternative.

    Two qualifications belong with that. The invariance holds for the revenue side and not for the cost side: fragmentation, section size, summer operations and the cost of a seat all move the $3,647, and they are the numbers this analysis most wants corrected. With fragmentation at zero the gap falls to about $1,945; with summer operations, staffing and transitions free, to about $718; at about $187,000 per built seat it closes entirely. And the objection about capacity runs the other way from what one might expect: if the campus has more room in fall and spring than the model assumes, the summer term avoids less construction, and the gap widens to about $6,090.

    Summer teaching: why A and B differ, and how C can be negative

    All three figures come from one mechanism: section size.

    The model charges summer instruction at an effective section size that shrinks as the off-cycle share of the student body grows: the fragmentation term described in Section 10 and priced for one department in Section 3.

    Fragmentation depends on the off-cycle share, not the off-cycle count. That is the whole explanation for the three numbers. Column A puts 500 students into summer while holding total headcount at 29,000, so the off-cycle share rises from 28.0% to 29.7%. Column B puts 500 students into summer but also raises headcount to 29,500, so the same 500 students lift the share only to 29.2%. Column C adds 500 students who never attend summer, so summer headcount does not change at all — but the denominator grows, and the share falls to 27.5%.

    A higher share means smaller effective sections, which means more faculty per student taught, applied to everyone already in summer as well as to the marginal students. So A costs slightly more than B ($117, or about 1.4%), and C shows a small saving of $103 because it makes the existing summer term marginally less fragmented. The −$103 does not mean costs decrease; it means the marginal cost of adding a single student decreases in this case.

    The check is to switch fragmentation off entirely. With the fragmentation slider at zero, the summer teaching row reads $7,767 in column A, $7,767 in column B, and exactly $0 in column C. The A–B gap and C's negative sign are, in their entirety, the fragmentation effect. Neither is a saving anyone can bank. C's −$103 is better read as a statement about summer: it is expensive partly because it is a large share of the whole, and diluting that share is the only thing traditional students do to it.

    Fall and spring lecturers: the half-term arithmetic and the hire's second term

    This row is the change in what the campus spends on lecturers in fall and spring, measured against today. Two mechanisms set it.

    First, how many regular-term seats each student occupies. A student attends two terms a year. A traditional student attends fall and spring, so occupies two regular-term seats. A student on a summer track attends one regular term and summer, so occupies one. Moving an existing student to summer therefore takes their regular-term seats from two to one; adding a new student on a summer track adds one; adding a new student on the traditional track adds two. Spread across the two regular terms, that is −0.5, +0.5 and +1.0 students per term. At 18 FTES per faculty member and 0.973 FTES per headcount, one additional student in each of the two regular terms requires 0.054 FTEF and costs $7,890 a year in lecturer salary and benefits. So the arithmetic before anything else is −$3,945, +$3,945 and +$7,890.

    Second, the new hires bring a regular term with them. Every tenure-line faculty member hired to staff summer is on a two-term contract, one term of which is summer. The other term is fall or spring — and that term absorbs teaching that would otherwise have been bought from lecturers. Each student added to summer generates 0.054 FTEF-terms of summer need, of which 60% is filled by new tenure-line hires (0.032 FTEF). That spare regular-term capacity is worth $2,367 a year of avoided lecturer cost. The credit applies to columns A and B, which both add a student to summer, and not to column C, which does not.

    What Column A is for

    Its role here is as an accounting decomposition and to provide clear comparison and illustration of the above. Adding a student on a summer track (B) is the same thing as adding a traditional student (C) and then moving one of their terms into summer (A). Column A isolates the cost of the calendar from the cost of growth, so that the two can be argued about separately. Read that way, the three columns say: growth costs roughly what it earns; the calendar is what costs money.

    There are also two circumstances in which the campus would find itself doing A for real, and both are worth stating because they are likely to arise.

    Minimum viable section sizes. A summer term built only from newly recruited students would be too thin to run in most majors. A major graduating forty students a year cannot field a viable upper-division sequence from the handful of new admits who chose a summer track. To open summer at all, existing students have to be moved into it, in numbers large enough to make sections work. That is column A, performed not for financial reasons but because the alternative is a summer term that cannot offer the courses students need. It is the most likely reason the campus would run column A at scale.

    The concurrent ceiling. Once fall and spring are at capacity, an existing student can only be accommodated by building space or by moving them to summer. In that situation column A does earn something: it frees half a concurrent seat per student moved, worth $2,439 a year in avoided construction, and its cost falls from $6,089 to $3,649. Still negative — but materially less so, and for the first time the summer term is doing something the campus could not otherwise buy.

    That second case points at the condition under which the whole proposition changes sign.

    The one condition under which summer pays

    Compare the two ways of measuring the same operation. B − C = $3,647 is moving one of a new student's two terms into summer, measured against a campus that is already over its ceiling at the margin, so it is credited with the construction it avoids. A = $6,089 is moving one of an existing student's two terms into summer, on a campus 60 seats under its ceiling, so it is credited with nothing.

    The difference is $2,442, which is precisely the annualized capital on half a seat: 0.5 × $75,000 × 6.505% = $2,439. The two measurements of the same operation differ by exactly the construction credit, and by nothing else.

    What summer actually produces, compared with buildingRelative to serving the same students in fall and spring, summer's financial product is avoided construction. It does not raise tuition, state funding or fees per student. What it does is let the campus serve a given number of students in less space. So, against the alternative of building that space, the calendar pays if and only if the space it saves is worth more than the teaching and operations it adds. Avoided construction is a comparison with an alternative that would otherwise be built; it is not income, and it does not cover an operating deficit unless that construction was genuinely going to happen.

    At the cautious scenario, what moving a term into summer avoids is $4,879 per seat-year, or $2,439 for the half-seat one student frees; what it costs is $6,089 per student, net of everything else. The second is about two and a half times the first. To close the gap, a new seat would have to cost about $187,000 rather than $75,000 — roughly $12,200 per seat-year. At that seat cost, and only at that seat cost, column A breaks even under a binding ceiling and columns B and C become exactly equal: at the margin, the campus would be indifferent between building a seat and moving a term to summer.

    The headcount arithmetic. The capacity figures in the table come from H = C ÷ (1 − σ/2), where H is distinct students in a year, C is the concurrent ceiling and σ is the off-cycle share. It follows from counting who is present in a regular term. Traditional students, H(1 − σ) of them, are present in both. Off-cycle students, Hσ of them, attend one regular term and summer — half in fall, half in spring — so Hσ/2 of them are present in any one regular term. Fall headcount is therefore H(1 − σ) + Hσ/2 = H(1 − σ/2). Setting that equal to the ceiling and solving gives the formula. At σ = 28%, that is 25,000 ÷ 0.86 = 29,070.

    One consequence catches the eye and is worth naming, because it looks like an error and is not. Subtracting the summer headcount from the annual headcount does not return 25,000, and the gap widens as summer fills: 26,882 − 3,763 = 23,119 in the first row, 30,864 − 11,728 = 19,136 in the last. The reason is that the summer cohort is not away in a body. Half of them are in fall and half are in spring, so subtracting all of them removes students who are on campus. H minus summer counts only the traditional students. The concurrent number is H minus half the summer cohort, which is 25,000 in every row, as it must be.

    Section size is the largest lever in the table. That is a real answer to the question, and it should be said plainly: at a teaching ratio half again as large, the plan works. It should be said equally plainly that this is not a financing adjustment. FTES per faculty is close to the definition of what Cal Poly is; it is the ratio that produces the class sizes, the project courses, the laboratory sections and the faculty contact that the campus recruits on and is accredited for. Changing it is a decision about the institution, and it belongs in a different conversation from this one.

    Nothing on this list is worth as much as the seat cost. The final row is there for contrast: the marginal threshold does more work than fragmentation, operations and services combined, and it is the only assumption that reverses the sign of the comparison without changing anything about how Cal Poly teaches.     Summary

    The plan's financial logic is that a fuller summer means fuller use of existing space. The model agrees with that logic and finds that it is worth $4,879 per seat per year, which is the annualized cost of the construction it avoids. It finds that the teaching, operations, services and transitions required to fill that seat in summer cost about $6,089 per student per year, and that the revenue attached is exactly zero, because a student's tuition, fees and state funding do not depend on which term they attend. Those three facts, taken together, produce every result in the ledger.

    They also identify what would change the answer: a documented all-in seat cost above roughly $187,000 at the margin and $228,000 for the whole plan, or summer sections that fill as well as fall's, or a decision about teaching ratios that is not really a budget decision at all. The numbers here are estimates and several of them are weak. The construction figure, the fragmentation factor and the summer operations estimate are the three most worth contesting, and each is a slider in the calculator. The purpose of this section is not to establish that YRO loses money; it is to identify which four or five numbers determine whether it does, so that the discussion can be about those.

    15. Method and assumptions

    Read the full section

    Scope and enrollment

    • The model is a steady-state annual ledger, incremental to a status quo of 23,400 headcount in fall and spring (FY2025-26 budget base; the Fall 2025 census was 23,245) at 0.973 FTES per student-term, plus the existing summer (1,100 FTES) treated as net zero.
    • The headcount and track-share sliders describe the full design; both are scaled toward today's figures by the share of enrollment in the programs on YRO (all of it once the remaining programs are set to join), so that growth and summer tracks apply only to participating programs while the rest of the campus runs fall and spring as today.
    • Students on off-cycle tracks attend two terms; those who do not realize the summer term are switched to fall and spring or, for the share set as lost, do not enrol at all, in which case the annual headcount falls short of target by that much and every line that scales with headcount falls with it.
    • Term enrollment: fall = spring = H·(1 − s·r/2); summer = H·s·r·λ.
    • Annual FTES = (fall + spring + summer) ÷ 2, the CSU convention.

    Revenue

    • Tuition and campus fees are charged per student-year regardless of terms attended; non-resident revenue is per non-resident student-year net of aid and regranted Opportunity Fee.
    • State funding applies to added resident FTES through two channels, new growth at the state share and reallocation at the reallocated rate, each a share of the added FTES, capped at 100% together.
    • Separately, the committed CSU enrollment target is today's resident FTES plus the chosen share of YRO's growth; if delivered resident FTES lands more than 5% below it, the reallocation share of the whole target is charged as a recurring loss of base funding.
    • Financial need: added students beyond today's 23% Pell mix carry the support and supplemental-aid increments; the State University Grant need of all added Pell students, at $2,790 each, is reported as a restricted flow and charged only for the share set as not yet recognized by the allocation.
    • Summer housing: added summer residents (summer students beyond today's session × the housed share, capped at the bed stock) × the net margin per resident-term enter as revenue, against the forgone conference income charged as a cost, so that auxiliary effects are consolidated. The calendar's auxiliary loss is a separate standing cost.

    Instruction

    • Instruction is priced per FTEF-term: volunteer tenure-line at 15 WTU × 1/30 salary + $150/unit + 7.9% intermittent benefits, with the volunteer pool split so that the service share of summer instruction can draw only on the fifth of tenure-line faculty in service departments; summer lecturers on appointments at half the annual salary with the full benefits load (or, on the extra-pay reading, 1/30 × salary per WTU + 7.9%); new hires at starting salary × (1 + premium) × (1 + fringe), each supplying one summer term and one regular term, with the number of lines scaled by 12 ÷ the direct-teaching WTU per tenure-line term; equity adjustments for existing faculty at count × starting salary × premium × (1 + fringe), recurring from year three of the ten-year view.
    • Regular-term lecturer need is recomputed each term against tenure-line capacity, so fall/spring lecturer savings (and idle tenure-line capacity) appear when regular terms shrink.
    • The fragmentation slider divides FTES-per-FTEF by (1 + fragmentation × min(1, off-cycle share ÷ 40%)) in the YRO scenario only, so the penalty scales with how many students are out of phase and reaches its full value at SEM's design.
    • Equity adjustments are charged only when premium-paid hires exist, for at most two existing faculty per new line.
    • The compliant staffing scenarios hold the summer lecturer share at the regular-term share; higher shares are available as diagnostics and flagged.

    Facilities and operations

    • Per-term transition cost is charged per year on the realized off-cycle headcount.
    • Facilities capital is annualized with a 30-year, 5% capital recovery factor (0.0651); the same factor prices built seats, charged to the traditional benchmark for every student above the cap and to YRO for any fall/spring overflow.
    • The standing costs of a third term (maintenance premium, forgone auxiliary income, administration) and the cooling retrofit phase in with the design share reached, and the rollout can be held after a chosen year, freezing the design share and those costs at that stage.

    Calendar and student outcomes

    • Calendar effects apply to the YRO scenario only: D/F pressure per year = headcount × 10 courses × affected share × D/F/W rise; the share absorbed by grading is removed unpriced, and the remainder becomes retakes, each a 3.5-unit seat taught at backfilled lecturer cost; retakes per graduate = 40 × affected share × rise × (1 − absorbed share), adding one-tenth of a year each; extra first-year attrition, likewise scaled by (1 − absorbed share), removes that share of a graduating cohort.
    • Instructional days per term drive the contact-hour warning and are not otherwise monetized; their consequence is priced through the D/F/W slider.
    • The calendar and student-outcome layer (D/F/W rise and retakes, calendar attrition, Fall–Summer and continuing off-cycle attrition, blocked minors and changes of major) is off in the steady state unless switched on, and is applied in the ten-year view to the first N cohort-years; the one-time redesign charge is always applied in year one.
    • Blocked minors and changes of major = off-cycle headcount × blocked share, of whom a quarter leave (removed from graduates) and three-quarters add half a year (added to time to degree).

    Throughput

    • Graduates = headcount ÷ (students per graduate + retake and blocked-student delays − 0.5 × acceleration share) − continuing off-cycle headcount × continuing attrition − half of the entering off-cycle cohort × Fall–Summer first-year attrition − calendar-driven first-year departures − blocked students who leave, where the entering cohort is headcount ÷ students per graduate.

    Full-cost admission

    • Full-cost admits (Section 13) are counted inside the annual headcount rather than added to it: the slider says how many of the added students are not state-supported. Each is priced at the net non-resident figure in place of resident tuition, with no state funding, and runs through the same track structure, instruction and service costs as every other student.

    The ten-year view and its dynamics

    • The ten-year view phases headcount and track shares in on a stepped schedule (about 30%, 55%, 80% and 100% of the design over years one to four, as each program's YRO cohorts accumulate, with the business cohorts a year ahead; programs set to join later start the same sequence after the chosen year), expenses the cooling retrofit as cash in proportion to the design share reached each year (the steady-state ledger annualizes it instead, which is the bridge between the two margins on the ten-year card), charges transition and redesign in year one, and charges startup and office fit-out as the hiring count rises; programs set to join later begin the same phase-in after the chosen year.
    • Dynamics: recurring costs grow at the cost-growth rate and tuition and fee revenue at the tuition-growth rate (state funding flat per FTES); after year four, summer realization is multiplied each year by (1 + decay); tenure lines are paid from the lead time ahead of the enrollment they serve (zero by default: searches run a year ahead, salaries start with the cohort) and are never reduced.
    • The plan-pays curve runs its preset with no decay, no lead, no calendar layer, and cost and tuition growth both at zero, unless the ten-year card is set to apply the current settings' dynamics to every curve.
    • Volunteer participation decays after year four at the realization-decay rate, and lost volunteer capacity is replaced by summer-obligated lines, which are sticky.
    • State funding of added FTES can be set to end after a chosen year, with the lines retained.
    • The applicant-pool slider declines the pool from the third year; two sensitivities, zero by default, reduce summer realization by a multiple of the pool's decline and raise the share of declined summer-track offers that are lost rather than backfilled, which the model applies as a continuous version of the enroll-elsewhere reading.
    • Present value and discounted payback use the discount-rate slider.

    Conventions

    • Section 14's revenue-invariance result is conditional on the compared students having the same residency, tuition, fee, aid and state-funding treatment.
    • Nominal dollars in the steady state.

    Not modeled: summer financial-aid cost beyond the tuition set-aside; effects on research productivity, sabbatical scheduling, or tenure density; housing and dining capital and rates, which are auxiliaries (their incremental net margin is a slider awaiting campus data); any change in admissions yield from offering summer-start admission; possible benefits of a third term for retakes and bottlenecks, and of fall or spring off-terms for co-ops, which would enter as sensitivities on the same footing as the downside layer; the cost of the "study abroad at the hangar" alternatives for off-cycle engineering students; and Cal Maritime.
