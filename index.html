SLOW the rush to YRO.

Cal Poly plans to launch year-round operations in fall 2028, a project that requires redesigning the calendar, the curriculum, housing, financial aid and staffing for every student. Two years out, no budget, no staffing plan and no financial-aid model has been published and the multi-year process of curriculum redesign has only just begun.

The authors of this webpage cannot claim to speak for the entire campus community. However, based on recent meetings of the Academic Senate, we are confident that the views expressed on this website accurately represent those of a large portion of the faculty. Academic Senate reports from every college on campus were uniformly and overwhelmingly against YRO and the rush to implement it.

1 · The clock

Twelve months to write the rules.

Applications for the first year-round classes open in October 2027. What has been published about the plan is shorter than the list of questions that remains.

Readiness has not been demonstrated against the March 12, 2027 catalog deadline or the October 2027 application opening. Blocks count items on the record below; they are not estimates of time.

Design to launch, on labeled milestones. The Academic Senate approved the semester calendar (AS-942-22) and the conversion curriculum-review process (AS-946-22) in May 2022, four years and three months before the first semester in August 2026. The two-of-three track design was first described publicly in October 2025, two years and ten months before the first 23 programs start in August 2028: a third shorter. The two milestones are not the same kind of event; one is a Senate approval, the other a public description. On an initiative-to-launch basis the two intervals are equal (directive October 2021 → August 2026; announcement September 2023 → August 2028), but the design now being built is the third in three years: a summer-start pilot (2023), a chosen summer or fall start (2024), and assigned two-of-three tracks (2025).

Design to launch. Initiative to launch, the two are equal (October 2021 → August 2026; September 2023 → August 2028); hover or tap for both.

Semester conversion took five years from directive to launch, three of them on curriculum: every course, including those that changed little, went through department, college, Senate-committee and Senate review under a process the Senate adopted in May 2022, with university-level submissions due in September 2023. YRO’s design-to-launch interval is a third shorter, for a project that touches almost every aspect of campus operations: academic, administrative, housing, financial, facilities, and the student experience. The catalog deadline for 2028–29 changes is March 12, 2027, seventeen months before launch. The Senate’s curriculum committee is charged this year with assessing YRO’s effects on review procedures; no resolution has yet come of it, and the Senate’s only action on a YRO calendar (AS-982-24, August 2024) asked that it be postponed. Whether re-sequencing a curriculum across three terms is harder than converting its units is a judgment; it is ours. And all of this comes before any opportunity to assess the impacts of the semester conversion just completed.

2 · The bill

Presented to the faculty as new revenue. Administration now tells faculty that self-support is part of paying for it.

The YRO plan was presented to the faculty as a source of revenue: enrollment growth whose surplus would fund the Teacher-Scholar Model and other high-impact practices. The arithmetic does not close that way. Faculty have since been told by the administration, in college YRO planning meetings, that self-support enrollment is part of the funding model. In plain terms, if that is the design:

Some California residents would be admitted to a California public university at prices set to recover full cost, so that we can afford to implement YRO.

No fee schedule, eligibility rule, student count or approval document has been published.

Existing published charges and the CSU's own marginal-cost figure, not a proposed YRO price. Today: Cal Poly's 2026–27 fee schedule, College of Engineering, 15 units a semester, $7,862 a semester for a resident. State share: the CSU's 2026–27 allocation memo sets the state's share of the marginal cost of instruction at $11,296 per full-time-equivalent student, which is what a cost-recovery fee would have to replace. Non-resident tuition: $471 a unit, from the same fee schedule, without the $4,402 Opportunity Fee charged to new non-residents, which has no demonstrated application to self-support students. The fee for the reported self-support pathway has not been published; its relationship to either reference point is unknown.

3 · Nobody does it this way

The schools it is compared to are not like it.

The universities the plan has been compared to: Georgia Tech, Northeastern, Cincinnati, Rensselaer, Waterloo, Dartmouth. Two rows: the population of each campus’s metropolitan area, and the enrollment growth the calendar is designed to produce.

Population of each campus's metropolitan or micropolitan statistical area, rounded (Waterloo: the Region of Waterloo). Cal Poly's is the San Luis Obispo–Paso Robles area, about 280,000, of which about 72,000 (the city, Los Osos and Morro Bay) are within a 45-minute bus ride of campus. Cal Poly has a smaller regional population than the large co-op comparators (Atlanta 22×, Boston 18×, Cincinnati 8×, Albany–Troy 3×, Waterloo 2×) and is the only one on the chart that assigns an entering class to unenrolled off semesters at admission: one student in four or five away every fall and every spring, from the first year. Its summer held about 9 percent of fall headcount in 2025; the design puts summer at 50 to 67 percent of fall headcount (40–50 percent of all students). Dartmouth's shared summer holds one class year; Rensselaer's Arch summer, required from 2019 until October 2025, the same. A smaller regional population does not by itself say where students will live or work; what the reviewed materials do not establish is whether suitable local jobs and housing exist at the scale required, or what support students would receive.

4 · We have done this before

The state has changed the rules for summer four times in 25 years.

  • 2001 The state pays CSU campuses to make summer a regular, state-supported term. Enrollment jumps.
  • 2004 The state cuts CSU's funded enrollment by about 20,000. Campuses are allowed to shut state-supported summer to stay within it. Seven do; Cal Poly's summer falls 83 percent.
  • 2009 Budget crisis. The Chancellor's Office lets campuses move summer to self-support and charge students full cost. Most of it moves.
  • 2025 Summer starts moving back to state support, because it now counts toward enrollment targets.

Cal Poly's own state-supported summer enrollment, in students,* across the last conversion.

2 · The bill

What a third semester costs.

Existing faculty cannot be compelled to teach a summer term, and historically have shown little interest in teaching during summer. A very large number of new tenure-track faculty will need to be hired, and those costs persist whether or not student summer enrollment persists. The money to carry them can not be supplied entirely by growth. Faculty have been told it requires Californians paying full cost.

How it would be paid for

The plan was presented to the faculty as a revenue source: growth whose surplus would fund the Teacher-Scholar Model and other high-impact practices. Within the assumptions and cases documented in a faculty financial analysis built on the public evidence, no configuration consistent with an equal-stature summer produces the $12–16 million a year the Teacher-Scholar Model would need, and most configurations lose money. The levers that close the gap in that analysis are state growth funding at marginal cost, indexed and sustained, or full-cost admission. This is a conditional model result, not proof that every feasible financing arrangement requires self-support.

Faculty have been told by the administration, in college YRO planning meetings, that self-support enrollment is part of the funding model: students admitted to the same major, on the same calendar, through Extended Education at a per-unit fee set to recover full cost, outside the state-supported count. CSU policy allows a self-support counterpart of an existing state-supported degree, subject to Chancellor's Office review and written approval, a cost-recovery budget with per-unit fees, and a showing that state-supported instruction is not supplanted (Education Code §89708). If this is the proposed pathway, the university should identify its approval status, cost-recovery budget, student charges, and how it would avoid supplanting. A Californian who pays $15,724 for two semesters of engineering today would pay on the order of $27,000 to $30,000 on either published full-cost reference point; the actual fee has not been published. The reported proposal is not specific to summer.

There are laws against self-support and state-supported students attending the same courses. The State Auditor found in 2016 that UC had disadvantaged California residents by admitting non-residents for revenue; a full-cost resident pathway reaches a similar end by a different route.

None of this has been proposed on the public record. The issue has been raised in Academic Senate but not fully discussed, nor is it clear all faculty have been told.

Why growth does not entirely pay for the summer

At the 25,000 cap, fall and spring are full. To seat one new student, two existing students have to give up a regular term and take summer instead. For each pair of students that enters a summer term, only one brings new revenue but both contribute to summer instructional costs and overhead. What that one is worth after teaching and serving all three is uncertain, and may not always be positive.

The administration's public position on cost is one sentence from May 2024: the investments required "will not be significantly out of line" with traditional growth. Since then the campus has hired 117 tenure-track faculty "partly to prepare," described the design, named 23 programs, and published no cost, revenue or staffing figure for a third semester.

What is published, and where

Strategic Enrollment Management described the design in October 2025: students enrol in two of three semesters, about 60 percent on the traditional track and 20–25 percent on each summer track, with no more than 85 percent of the student body on campus in any term and 40–50 percent in summer; its target is 25,000 students present on campus in a term by 2030, the Master Plan ceiling. The 2023 announcement spoke of a 600-student pilot and "possibly 6,000" summer students. Academic Affairs lists the 23 programs. None of them is a cost or a revenue, and no cost or revenue figure has been published alongside them.

What follows from them is not published either. If fall and spring are at the ceiling and 40–50 percent of students spend one of them away, the number of distinct students enrolled across a year rises to 31,250 under a 60/20/20 track split and 33,333 under 50/25/25, the two readings of the reported shares that sum to 100; the faculty financial analysis’s central case, about 29,000, assumes the summer tracks fill only partly. Every cost that scales with people, from advising to housing to the city's rental market, scales with that larger number, and the administration has not said what it is.

The only summer with public books

Cal Poly's 2025–26 operating budget carries summer 2025 as its own column: 2,078 people enrolled, 1,099 students full-time equivalent, $12.87 million in revenue against $12.91 million in expense. That summer was taught by volunteers at the quarter-system rate and ran at the overhead of a campus already open, and it roughly broke even. Institutional Research projects summer 2026 about 30 percent smaller from the move to two sessions. The one summer the campus has published accounts for is a small voluntary term that just clears zero, and it is about to shrink.

The lines

Summer instruction has to be taught by someone. The bargaining agreement makes summer teaching voluntary for academic-year faculty, and the administration has said faculty "will not be required to teach in summer." An equal-stature summer with the same tenure density as fall and spring therefore means new tenure-track lines whose appointments include summer. Each dot below is one such line.

About 240 new tenure lines at the faculty financial analysis’s central settings; its scenarios span roughly 150 to 250. Assumptions: 40 percent of summer instruction by lecturers, the regular-term share, the rest by tenure-line faculty once the volunteer pool is exhausted; a $115,000 starting salary, a 10 percent recruiting premium and Cal Poly's 64.4 percent benefits rate, so each line costs about $208,000 a year, roughly $50 million a year of base budget for 240. The 117 tenure-track faculty hired in 2023–25 "partly to prepare" are shown filled for scale only; how many of them carry summer obligations, and how many count against the 240, is unpublished.

A tenure line is a thirty-year commitment. The present value of 240 of them at 5 percent is roughly three-quarters of a billion dollars, before startup packages that run to six figures each in engineering and science. If summer enrollment falls short, the lines are hard to shed: the bargaining agreement permits layoffs for lack of work or funds, with tenured faculty last in order, and the CSU has used that authority rarely, in 2025 only alongside program eliminations at Sonoma State. A line hired for summer 2028 could create decades of continuing costs, and shedding it has academic and human consequences the plan has not priced. The shortfall comes instead from the parts of the budget that can move: lecturer sections, operating budgets, deferred maintenance, and the discretionary programs the new revenue was supposed to fund. The same hires directed at fall and spring would lift tenure density from about 63 percent toward the level the Senate has asked for, for the same money. That is the cost of the calendar, and no revenue argument changes it.

Five direction changes in state summer policy in 25 years. A line hired in 2028 is still on the payroll in the 2050s.

The off term produces no tuition

A student on a two-of-three track pays tuition for two semesters a year, the same as a student on the traditional track, so moving an existing student onto a summer track brings no new tuition and adds a summer seat to teach. At the co-op universities the plan is compared to, a student on a work term is in a paid position that a co-op office helped them find, is often still registered, and is supported. Under the Cal Poly plan the off-semester student is not enrolled at all, pays nothing, and has no office arranging anything.

Financial aid at the off term

In May 2024 the administration said aid would cover a full academic year regardless of start term. That statement was made under the quarter calendar, for a design in which students chose a summer or fall start. Nothing has been published about aid under a two-of-three semester design: whether Year-Round Pell covers the summer semester, how Cal Grant and institutional scholarships treat a spring off term, work-study for a student whose off term is spring, and disbursement timing. Worked examples for each track and aid type would answer them. These are the questions the co-op universities answer with a paid work term arranged through a co-op office, which the Cal Poly plan does not include.

The state is already discounting the numbers

The Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) tracks CSU summer enrollment by funding type. Between 2022–23 and 2025–26, campuses moved summer courses back under state support, and the LAO warned the Legislature that CSU's reported enrollment growth is overstated by that shift. Counting courses shifted from self-support to state support as new enrollment is what the LAO flagged; new summer instruction is not reclassification, and the administration's model should separate the two.

Costs with no line

  • Summer housing and dining for the 40–50 percent of students on campus in summer, in a term when sorority chapter houses are generally closed and most fraternity houses are empty.
  • Two more orientations, two more advising cycles, two more move-in operations, and two more first-year programs a year.
  • Attrition. In the faculty financial analysis’s modeled scenario, a student who leaves after a thin first semester takes the gross tuition of three further years with them.

Cal Poly's plan against the places it is compared to.

The president has cited Dartmouth and Waterloo as models, and the campus has studied the co-op universities. Of the institutions reviewed, none assigns an entering class to unenrolled off semesters at admission. The one that required a summer semester of every student ended the requirement in October 2025. Why place matters

Co-op students on a work term are in a paid position that an office helped them find, are often still registered, and are supported; Cincinnati tells its co-op students to be willing to work anywhere in the country. A Cal Poly student on an assigned off semester is not enrolled, has no job the university arranged, and has no office to turn to. Cal Poly also has a smaller regional population than the large co-op comparators: the San Luis Obispo–Paso Robles metropolitan area is about 280,000, and about 72,000 people live within a 45-minute bus ride of campus. The reviewed materials do not establish whether suitable local jobs and affordable housing would be available at the scale required, or what support students would receive during assigned off semesters. The scale is the other half. In 2015 the Legislative Analyst's Office found only two CSU campuses with state-supported summer enrollment above 10 percent of fall, and Cal Poly was not one of them. Dartmouth

Hanover, NH · D-Plan since 1972

Every sophomore is on campus for the same summer. Off-terms are chosen individually around that shared core; the class is kept together for its first year and its sophomore summer. Dartmouth students told a Rensselaer delegation that the D-Plan helps their Greek houses because underclassmen take leadership roles while upperclassmen are away. The Cal Poly plan divides each entering class by track at admission, so that shared core does not exist.

Rensselaer

Troy, NY · The Arch, required 2019–2025

The one peer that required a summer semester of every student, from 2019 until October 2025, when the requirement was dropped; a 2019 Student Senate survey had found 78 percent of respondents unsatisfied. In 2016 the university told Greek-affiliated students they would live in residence halls for the Arch summer; the Greek council called it financially harmful to the houses. In 2025 the vice provost described summer Greek housing as still under evaluation, citing safety, summer dining and housing revenue. The housing question was still open when the requirement ended.

Georgia Tech

Atlanta, GA · co-op since 1912

Georgia Tech's summer is a shorter session, not a third semester equivalent to fall and spring. Co-op is optional, alternates work and school terms at the student's choice, and is run by a standing office in a metropolitan area of six million people. It is a program at the margin of a conventional calendar, not a third semester.

The Fall–Summer student experience: a scenario

A Fall–Summer first-year arrives in August with the rest of the class, joins a chapter or a team in September, and leaves in December. They are away all spring. They return in May to a summer campus holding 40–50 percent of students and none from the traditional track, where most fraternity houses are empty and sorority chapter houses are generally closed, and where the officers who recruited them are gone. The design assumes this student stays. We expect the first organized request to switch tracks to come from the chapters, the teams and the friend groups that want to be on the same calendar, and the track they will ask for is the traditional one.

4 · We have done this before

This is not the first foray into boosting summer use of campus

In 2001 the Legislature funded year-round operations at the CSU and fifteen campuses converted summer to a state-supported term. What happened next is the best evidence available about what happens to a Cal Poly summer term over time.

The swings

  • 1999. Before the initiative: about 17,000 state-supported and 12,800 self-supported summer students systemwide.
  • 2001. Fifteen campuses convert summer to a state-supported term. Summer 2001 reaches 42,146 students across 21 campuses, and the CSU serves 1,977 more students than the Legislature had funded.
  • 2003. State-supported summer peaks at over 18,000 students, annualized.
  • 2004. The state cuts CSU's funded enrollment by about 20,000; the CSU permits campuses "to reduce or eliminate their state-supported summer offerings." Seven campuses report zero. Systemwide summer enrollment falls to 29,787 students. Cal Poly falls from 2,364 to 412.
  • 2006. Summer recovers to 42,741 students, 101 percent of 2001. Cal Poly is back at 2,152. The reason is funding, not demand: the state resumed paying for enrollment growth in 2005–06 (about 8,100 students) and 2006–07, the CSU was running below its new targets, and summer was the quickest way to add funded enrollment. The $7 million appropriated for conversion had stayed in the CSU base throughout.
  • 2009–11. The fiscal crisis creates authority and incentive to move summer into self-support. Long Beach, which reported 3,620 state-supported summer students in 2006, offers 18 state-supported summer seats out of more than 17,500 in 2010–11 and 2011–12.
  • 2015. The Legislature amends Education Code §89708 to ban supplanting: a campus may not reduce state-supported offerings while expanding self-support versions, and may not require a state-supported student to take a self-support course to graduate.
  • 2022–23. Thirty percent of resident summer instruction is state-supported systemwide.
  • 2023–26. Enrollment-target incentives move summer back toward state support; the share reaches 68 percent, and the LAO warns that reported growth is overstated by the shift.
  • 2025, Cal Poly. Summer 2025 enrolled 2,078 people, 1,099 students full-time equivalent, state-supported, with $12.87 million of tuition revenue against about $12.7 million of expense. The campus projects summer 2026 about 30 percent smaller.

Cal Poly's own summer, then and now

Cal Poly ran a state-supported summer quarter from the late 1960s. In 2001 it enrolled 2,364 summer students; in 2004, after the system let campuses cut state-supported summer, 412; in 2006, 2,152. The campus has published no summer series for 2007 through 2024. By 2015 the LAO put its state-supported summer below 10 percent of fall. In 2025 the operating budget shows 2,078 people enrolled and 1,099 full-time-equivalent students, less than half the 2001 level, carried entirely on tuition with no General Fund line, and within two percent of break-even. For 2026 the campus projects a 30 percent drop as summer compresses to two sessions on the semester calendar.

So the summer that YRO would turn into a semester of equal stature is one that the campus has let shrink by more than half since 2001, that the state has not paid for in the budget's own presentation, and that is about to shrink again. The 2001 conversion came with state money attached, $19.9 million for CSU fee reductions and a share of $33.1 million for conversion, and with enrollment-growth conditions. No appropriation has been announced or scheduled for this one.

What flips, and what remains constant

Summer funding, fee authority, enrollment credit and classification have each changed direction within a budget cycle or two, in both directions. The commitments a year-round campus makes do not.

Five direction changes in summer policy in 25 years; the longest interval between two of them was fourteen years. A bond issued in 2026 is still being repaid in the 2050s.

Thou shalt not compel a California resident to pay as a nonresident

A campus shall not require a state-supported matriculated student to enroll in a special session course in order to fulfill a graduation requirement for a state-supported degree program.Education Code §89708(d), as amended by AB 716, Chapter 252, Statutes of 2015

Any design that routes state-supported students into a self-support summer they need for degree progress is prohibited on its face. A state-supported summer does not engage the statute, which is why the question of which kind of summer Cal Poly is planning is not a detail.

The lesson

The 2001 initiative did not fail; it worked, was cut, recovered, was reclassified, and is being reclassified again. Its record is of volatility, not collapse. A plan that assigns seventeen-year-olds to a summer track, builds housing for them and hires faculty to teach them needs the summer term's funding, classification and enrollment credit to hold still for decades. Nothing in the record suggests they will.
